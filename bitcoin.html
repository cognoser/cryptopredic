BTC · Bitcoin

Bitcoin: the original digital asset

Bitcoin is a peer-to-peer payment network and a scarce digital asset. It is also the market's main reference point: when Bitcoin moves sharply, the rest of crypto usually pays attention.

What Bitcoin is trying to solve

Bitcoin lets people transfer value on a public network without asking a bank or a single company to approve every transaction. The network uses proof-of-work mining to order transactions and protect its shared history.

That design comes with trade-offs. Bitcoin is intentionally conservative, and it cannot offer every feature or speed that newer networks promise. Its appeal is partly the simplicity of the base layer, the size of its user community, and the difficulty of changing the rules without broad agreement.

Why people hold or use it

  • As a long-term bet on digital scarcity and open settlement.
  • For transfers that do not depend on a single financial intermediary.
  • As a liquid market used to measure wider crypto risk appetite.

Risks worth taking seriously

Bitcoin can lose a large amount of value quickly. Self-custody creates responsibility for keys and backups, while exchange custody creates dependence on a third party. Regulation, leverage, liquidity, mining economics, and energy policy can all affect the market.

The Satoshi story

On October 31, 2008, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto posted the Bitcoin white paper to the Cryptography Mailing List. The first message was technical and modest: it described a peer-to-peer electronic cash system and invited readers to look at the design.

Satoshi later helped release the software and worked with early contributors, including Hal Finney. The identity behind the name remains unconfirmed. The last public forum message is generally dated December 2010, while a later email exchange with early developers is usually treated as the last known private communication. The distinction matters because “last message” can mean different things.

The mystery has become part of Bitcoin's culture. A bronze, faceless Satoshi statue in Budapest reflects the idea that the creator is less important than the open network that followed. It is a memorable symbol, not evidence about Satoshi's identity.
